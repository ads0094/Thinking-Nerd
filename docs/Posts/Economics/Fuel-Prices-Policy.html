Free Public Transport

A month of free travel, and a harder question beneath it

Fuel Shock • Cost of Living • Public Policy

Victoria’s decision to make public transport free from 31 March to 30 April 2026 is politically easy to understand. Petrol prices were spiking, the ACCC had shifted to weekly fuel monitoring during the recent Middle East conflict, and the Allan Government wanted a visible, immediate way to ease household pressure. The policy covers trains, trams, buses and V/Line services across Victoria, with the legal change formalised through Amending Conditions No. 1/2026.

The case for the policy is stronger than some critics admit.

First, it is simple. Governments often reach for targeted subsidies in a crisis, but those can be slow, bureaucratic and badly timed. Free fares are the opposite: easy to explain, easy to implement, and immediately visible to households. Victoria’s own framing was blunt and practical: “take pressure off the pump”; and as a short-run crisis measure that logic is not absurd.

Second, it provides real cost-of-living relief for existing public transport users; it is an emergency affordability measure. For people who already commute by train, tram or bus, free fares are a direct and immediate transfer. They do not need to apply for anything, prove hardship, or wait for a rebate. That makes the policy especially attractive in a shock where timing matters.

Third, the fiscal cost is meaningful but not enormous in the context of Victoria’s already heavily subsidised network. The government’s public estimate was a little over $70 million in foregone fare revenue for the month, while the Victorian Auditor-General reported $736 million in fare revenue in 2024–25 against $3.2 billion paid by the department to operators. In other words, fares already fund only a minority of system costs. Waiving them for a month does not “remove” the cost of public transport; it shifts more of that cost onto general revenue. But for a temporary measure, that shift is fiscally manageable.

But the case against the policy is also serious.

The main economic weakness is that free public transport is a poor instrument if the objective is specifically to cut petrol use. The key question is not whether ridership rises. It almost certainly does. The key question is who those extra passengers are. If they are mostly people who would otherwise have walked, cycled, delayed a trip, or taken public transport anyway, then the fuel-saving effect is small. Fare cuts tend to produce more ridership more reliably than they produce large reductions in car traffic.

The Tallinn (Estonia) fare-free reform was associated with an increase of about 14% in public transport trips, but the gains did not come only from former drivers; some came from reduced walking as well. That is useful, but it is not the same thing as saying free fares are a powerful congestion or fuel-saving policy.

That distinction matters for Victoria. If the policy’s real goal is household relief, it is reasonably well targeted toward existing PT users. If the goal is fuel conservation, it is much less precise. Outer-suburban and regional households that are most exposed to petrol costs are often also the households least well served by frequent public transport. That means the policy can be progressive for some users while still missing many of the people most dependent on driving. The trade-off here is simple: inner-metro users with strong rail and tram access are likely to benefit more than households in car-dependent areas.

There is also a practical transport problem. Free fares can increase crowding faster than service capacity can adjust. V/Line itself warned of busy services during the free-travel period, and the legal setup created a split system in which metro travel was effectively open access while some regional services still needed free tickets or booking management. That is good enough for an emergency month, but not a clean long-term design. If crowding worsens reliability, the policy can end up degrading the very system it is trying to encourage people to use.

There is also a data problem, which sounds boring but is actually important. Because tapping on and off was no longer required for many users, the government loses some of the behavioural data that would normally help it understand where loads are building and whether the policy actually shifted people out of cars. For a one-month emergency this is tolerable. For a longer extension it becomes much harder to defend a policy if you cannot properly measure its effects.

So what is the right verdict?

As a temporary emergency response, the policy makes sense. It is fast, visible, legally clear, and likely to deliver genuine short-run relief to many Victorians. On those terms, it is defensible. The Premier’s office was explicit that it was temporary, and that is important. A one-month shock response should not be judged by the same standard as a permanent fare reform.

As a longer-term transport policy, though, the case is much weaker. If the government wanted to extend it beyond April, the burden of proof would rise sharply. It would need to show not just that ridership went up, but that car use fell meaningfully, that crowding remained manageable, and that the fiscal trade-off beat alternative uses of the money. Your paper is persuasive on this point: beyond a short emergency window, targeted measures would probably perform better, especially service upgrades in outer suburbs, targeted relief for fuel-exposed households, and corridor-specific interventions aimed at actual car substitution rather than headline fare abolition.

The bottom line is that free public transport for a month is a good politics, decent relief, weak structural reform policy. It helps some households immediately. It may modestly lift ridership. But by itself it is unlikely to transform congestion, emissions or fuel demand. For that, the harder work remains what it has always been: better service, better coverage, and policies aimed directly at car dependence rather than just the price of a tram ticket.
