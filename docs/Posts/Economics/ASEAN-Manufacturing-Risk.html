The Strait of Hormuz and Southeast Asia’s Manufacturing Risk

Why manufacturers need to plan for energy disruption.

Energy • Freight • Manufacturing

Published: May 4, 2026

Southeast Asia’s factories are exposed in a way that is easy to understate.

The region is usually presented as one of the winners of supply-chain diversification: competitive labour costs, deepening export capacity, and a growing role as an alternative manufacturing base to China. Yet the Iran war has exposed a more uncomfortable dependency. Much of this industrial strength still relies on energy, shipping and petrochemical inputs whose prices are shaped far from the factory floor.

The Strait of Hormuz is where that dependency becomes visible. In 2025, around 20 million barrels a day of oil and oil products moved through the waterway, roughly a quarter of global seaborne oil trade. Around 80 per cent of that flow was headed to Asia. The same corridor also carried nearly one-fifth of global LNG trade.

The problem for manufacturers is not only that oil becomes more expensive, but that the shock can travel through several cost lines at once: fuel, freight, insurance, petrochemical inputs, delivery times and factory confidence.

This makes Hormuz more than a distant geopolitical risk for Southeast Asian manufacturers. It is a pressure point inside the region’s manufacturing model.

The first hit is energy

The strain is already showing up in manufacturing surveys. The Purchasing Managers’ Index, or PMI, is a monthly gauge of factory conditions, with readings above 50 pointing to expansion and readings below 50 pointing to contraction. A falling PMI does not necessarily mean factories are shrinking, but it does suggest that momentum is weakening.

Across ASEAN, that weakening has arrived alongside rising costs. S&P Global reported that the regional manufacturing PMI fell to 51.8 in March from February’s record 53.8, while input-price inflation reached its highest level since October 2022. Output-price inflation also accelerated to a three-year high. In practical terms, manufacturers were still expanding, but they were doing so with dearer fuel, transport, materials and a growing need to pass costs on to customers.

Vietnam shows why this matters. Its manufacturing PMI fell to 51.2 from 54.3, meaning the sector was still growing but losing speed. At the same time, selling prices rose at the fastest pace in almost 15 years, with firms citing higher freight, fuel and transport costs. This is the uncomfortable part of the shock: factories are not only paying more to produce; they may also be losing the demand strength that helps them absorb or pass on those costs.

The Philippines faced a similar squeeze. Its PMI fell to 51.3 from 54.6 as input prices spiked, while Reuters reported that Manila declared a national energy emergency in March. The country imports almost all of its crude from the Middle East, making its manufacturing base especially vulnerable to fuel-price volatility.

Manufacturers producing electronics, garments, food products, packaging, components and chemicals are therefore facing a more difficult operating environment: higher input costs, softer factory momentum and less room to protect margins. Vietnam’s policy response suggests the pressure had already moved beyond market prices. The government cut import tariffs on fuel and several petrochemical feedstocks to zero for a period, moved to stabilise retail prices, and gave state energy firms more flexibility to secure crude and diversify supply.

Then the factory maths changes

The second hit is less visible, but just as damaging: freight, insurance and working capital.

ASEAN economic ministers warned in May that Hormuz disruptions were sharply increasing freight, insurance and logistics costs, while also lifting fertiliser prices and threatening regional supply chains. Argus Media reported last month that extra war-risk premiums for tankers and bulk carriers in the Gulf were around 1 per cent. For manufacturers, this matters because the added cost does not sit neatly inside shipping. It spreads into resin, chemicals, diesel, packaging, transport, generator fuel and the final trip to port.

This is where the factory maths begins to change. A firm may not import oil directly from the Gulf, but it can still pay more for the materials, energy and logistics that sit behind production. The shock, in other words, raises not only the price of fuel, but also the cost of making, moving and selling goods.

Thailand shows why the pressure can be difficult to read in real time. Thai government official Poonpong Naiyanapakorn, Director-General of the Trade Policy and Strategy Office within the Ministry of Commerce, has said that around 50 per cent of the country’s energy imports come from the Middle East, while also flagging higher prices for fertilisers and plastic pellets. Yet Thailand’s March PMI still rose to 54.1. On the surface, that points to continued expansion. Underneath, however, S&P Global reported that business expectations had fallen to their weakest level in almost four-and-a-half years, suggesting that factories can remain busy in the short term while becoming more cautious about costs, orders and future production.

Indonesia shows a different version of the same vulnerability. Although it has more domestic energy resources than some neighbours, officials said in March that a quarter of its crude imports and 30 per cent of its LPG imports come from the Middle East. Two state energy vessels were also stuck in Hormuz at one point. Indonesia’s manufacturing PMI then fell to 50.1 from 53.8, leaving the sector barely above the expansion threshold, while output declined and delivery delays worsened.

Taken together, Thailand and Indonesia show how Hormuz risk can appear in more than one form. In one case, output can remain resilient while confidence weakens; in the other, energy exposure, shipping disruption and softer factory activity can arrive together. That is the manufacturing problem: the shock does not need to close factories immediately to damage margins, delay inputs or make production decisions more cautious.

What smart manufacturers do next

The bigger question is not whether Southeast Asian manufacturing can survive a Hormuz shock, because it almost certainly can. The more important question is what kind of manufacturing model remains competitive when energy, shipping and imported inputs are no longer cheap, stable or easy to plan around.

The World Bank warned in April that a sustained 50 per cent increase in fuel prices could reduce household incomes across the region by 3 to 4 per cent, while its Commodity Markets Outlook projected a 24 per cent rise in energy prices in 2026 and a 31 per cent rise in fertiliser prices. Brent crude was forecast to average US$86 a barrel under the baseline scenario, but could rise to US$115 if the shock deepens.

This matters for manufacturers because the pressure comes from both sides of the market. If households lose purchasing power, demand for manufactured goods weakens; if fuel, freight, fertiliser and petrochemical inputs become more expensive at the same time, the cost of producing those goods rises. A factory may therefore face weaker orders and higher production costs simultaneously, which is precisely the combination that compresses margins and makes investment decisions more cautious.

The response has to move beyond short-term cost control. Manufacturers need to know which inputs are genuinely critical, where those inputs come from, and how easily they can be replaced if freight routes become unreliable or Gulf-linked energy prices move sharply. Buying feedstocks from more than one region, writing freight and energy cost pass-through clauses into contracts, and holding larger inventories of hard-to-replace materials may look inefficient in normal times, but they become a form of insurance when shipping and energy markets are unstable.

Energy efficiency also becomes more strategic in this environment. Every avoided kilowatt-hour reduces exposure to volatile fuel and power prices, while more efficient logistics, machinery and production scheduling can soften the impact of external shocks that firms cannot control. The point is not simply to reduce emissions or operating costs, but to make the production model less vulnerable to sudden increases in energy prices.

Manufacturers also need to look beyond their direct suppliers. Tier-two suppliers are the firms that supply a company’s immediate suppliers, and they are often where hidden vulnerabilities sit. A manufacturer may not buy directly from the Gulf, but its packaging supplier may depend on resin, its chemicals supplier may rely on petrochemical feedstocks, or its logistics provider may be exposed to diesel and shipping insurance costs. In that sense, Hormuz risk can enter the factory through the second or third layer of the supply chain rather than through the factory gate itself.

This is where the old “China+1” strategy needs updating. China+1 refers to the practice of keeping production in China while adding another manufacturing base, often in Southeast Asia, to reduce dependence on one country. But diversification by location is not enough if the alternative production base still depends on fragile energy routes, imported petrochemicals and volatile shipping costs. The next version of China+1 will need to ask not only where goods are assembled, but how exposed that production is to energy shocks, freight disruption and input-price volatility.

For Southeast Asian manufacturers, Hormuz is therefore not just a crisis to monitor. It is a reminder that competitiveness depends not only on wages, export demand and industrial policy, but also on the resilience of the energy and logistics systems sitting underneath the factory floor.

The takeaway

Hormuz risk is not a distant naval drama for Southeast Asia. It is a manufacturing cost problem, a procurement problem and, if the shock lasts, a demand problem too.

The factories best placed to handle it will not simply be those with the cheapest labour, but those with enough supplier flexibility, energy resilience and working-capital discipline to absorb expensive surprises without freezing up.
