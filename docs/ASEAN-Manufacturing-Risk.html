The Strait of Hormuz and Southeast Asia’s Manufacturing Risk

Why manufacturers need to plan for energy disruption.

Energy • Freight • Manufacturing

A chokepoint thousands of kilometres away can still punch straight through a factory’s profit margin in Southeast Asia. 

The Strait of Hormuz carried about 20 million barrels a day of oil and oil products in 2025, roughly a quarter of global seaborne oil trade with around 80 per cent of that flow was headed to Asia. The same corridor also carried nearly one-fifth of global LNG trade.

When tension rises in the Gulf, the effect does not stay in the Middle East; it travels through shipping routes, fuel bills, petrochemical prices, insurance premiums and factory input costs. For manufacturers in Asia, the Strait of Hormuz blockade is a fuel, freight, feedstock and production-cost issue.

Southeast Asia is especially exposed because the region is already becoming more dependent on imported energy. The International Energy Agency estimates that Southeast Asia’s oil import bill is around US$130 billion and is likely to keep rising under current policy settings. The region is also expected to become a net gas importer by the late 2020s. This means any disruption around Hormuz can quickly become a manufacturing problem.

The first hit is energy

The pressure is already visible in manufacturing surveys. S&P Global reported that the first effects of the Middle East conflict were showing across ASEAN economies. The regional manufacturing PMI dropped to 51.8 in March from February’s record 53.8. Input-price inflation reached its highest level since October 2022, while output-price inflation accelerated to a three-year high.

In Vietnam, the manufacturing PMI fell to 51.2 from 54.3, while selling prices rose at the fastest pace in almost 15 years. Firms pointed to higher freight, fuel and transport costs. In the Philippines, the PMI fell to 51.3 from 54.6 as input prices spiked. Reuters reported that Manila declared a national energy emergency in March, with the country importing almost all of its crude from the Middle East.

For firms making electronics, garments, food products, packaging, components or chemicals, this creates a difficult combination: weaker demand, higher input costs and less room to absorb either.

The Vietnamese government cut import tariffs on fuel and several petrochemical feedstocks to zero for a period, moved to stabilise retail prices, and gave state energy firms more flexibility to secure crude and diversify supply. Governments usually do not intervene like that unless the pressure is already reaching the real economy.

Then the factory maths changes

The second hit is less visible but just as damaging: freight, insurance and working capital.

ASEAN economic ministers warned in May that Hormuz disruptions were sharply increasing freight, insurance and logistics costs. They also pointed to higher fertiliser prices and risks to regional supply chains. 

Argus Media reported in April that extra war-risk premiums for tankers and bulk carriers in the Gulf were around 1 per cent. For manufacturers, those costs show up in different shapes and forms. They affect resin, chemicals, diesel, packaging, transport, generator fuel and the final trip to port. This is the part that often gets underestimated. The shock does not only raise the price of oil: it raises the price of making, moving and selling goods.

Thailand is a useful example. Thai government official Poonpong Naiyanapakorn, Director-General of the Trade Policy and Strategy Office (TPSO) within the Ministry of Commerce, said in April that around 50 per cent of the country’s energy imports come from the Middle East. It also flagged higher prices for fertilisers and plastic pellets. Yet Thailand’s March PMI still rose to 54.1. That is exactly why this risk is tricky: output can look fine for a month or two while confidence weakens underneath. S&P Global said Thai business expectations fell to their weakest level in almost four-and-a-half years. This means the factories can stay busy and still become more nervous.

Indonesia sits somewhere in the middle. It has more domestic energy resources than some neighbours, but officials said in March that a quarter of its crude imports and 30 per cent of its LPG imports come from the Middle East. Two state energy vessels were also stuck in Hormuz at one point. Indonesia’s manufacturing PMI then dropped to 50.1 from 53.8, with output falling and delivery delays worsening.

That is the Hormuz risk in one snapshot, with physical vulnerability, higher input costs and factory disruption landing together.

What smart manufacturers do next

The bigger question is not whether Southeast Asian manufacturing can survive a Hormuz shock, because it can. The real question is what kind of manufacturing model works when energy and shipping are no longer cheap, smooth and predictable.

The World Bank warned in April that a sustained 50 per cent increase in fuel prices could reduce household incomes across the region by 3 to 4 per cent. Its Commodity Markets Outlook also projected a 24 per cent rise in energy prices in 2026 and a 31 per cent rise in fertiliser prices, with Brent crude averaging US$86 a barrel in the baseline scenario and potentially US$115 if the shock deepens.

In other words manufacturers can be squeezed from both sides. Consumers have less spending power, while factories pay more for inputs to produce..

Manufacturers need to buy critical feedstocks from more than one region. They need contract clauses that allow for freight and energy cost pass-through. They may need to hold more inventory for materials that are difficult to replace quickly. Energy efficiency also becomes more valuable because every avoided kilowatt-hour reduces exposure to volatile prices.

They also need to pay closer attention to tier-two suppliers, especially those linked to petrochemicals, packaging, fertilisers and transport. A company may think it has limited exposure to the Gulf, only to discover that a supplier’s supplier depends on fuel, resin or shipping routes affected by Hormuz.

The old “China+1” manufacturing strategy now needs an energy-security chapter.

The takeaway

Hormuz risk is not a distant naval drama for Southeast Asia. It is a manufacturing cost problem, a procurement problem and, if the shock lasts, a demand problem too.

The factories that handle it best will not simply be the ones with the cheapest labour. They will be the ones that can manage expensive surprises without freezing up.
